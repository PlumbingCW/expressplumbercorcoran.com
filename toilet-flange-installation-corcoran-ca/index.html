Installing a Toilet Flange: A Guide for Corcoran, CA Homes

A toilet that rocks or leaks at the base often has a flange problem. Here is how to tell and what the fix involves.

Written by the Corcoran Express Plumbing team · Published 2026

A toilet flange is the ring that bolts the toilet to the floor and connects the bowl to the drain. When it cracks, rusts or sits too low, the wax seal cannot hold, and water seeps out at the base. In Corcoran, CA homes that leak is often noticed first as a soft spot in the floor or a smell in the bathroom.

This guide explains what a flange is, how installation works and when replacing one is a job for a plumber. It also covers the repair rings that can save a damaged flange without cutting the floor.

What a toilet flange does

The flange sits on top of the floor and over the drain pipe. A wax ring or foam seal fits between the flange and the bottom of the toilet. Two closet bolts hold the toilet down, and the flange keeps them in place.

A flange can be plastic, brass, cast iron or galvanized steel. Plastic and brass last well. Older cast iron flanges rust and can crack. The material tells you what repair is possible.

Flange materials compared

Your plumber can identify the material when the toilet is pulled.

Plastic (PVC or ABS)GoodCracked earsRepair ring or new flange

Signs the flange has failed

A rocking toilet is the most common sign. Others include water around the base after flushing, a sewer smell, a floor that feels soft and a toilet that will not stay tightened. A flange that sits below the finished floor level lets the seal fail even if the toilet is new.

If the floor has been wet for a long time, the subfloor may be damaged. A plumber checks the wood around the flange before any new parts go in.

Quick checks before you pull the toilet

  • Does the toilet move when you push the bowl?
  • Is there water or staining around the base?
  • Is the floor soft near the toilet?
  • Has the floor been recently re-tiled or raised?

How a new flange is installed

Replacement starts with shutting off the water, draining the tank and bowl and removing the toilet. The old wax is scraped away and the flange is inspected. A damaged plastic flange is unscrewed and a new one is set on the drain pipe.

The flange must sit flat on the finished floor and be screwed to solid wood. If the drain pipe is cracked or rusted, the work moves to the drain. A new wax ring, new closet bolts and a level toilet complete the job.

Repair rings and when they work

A repair ring is a metal or plastic plate that fits over a cracked flange and is screwed into the floor. It restores a solid base for the bolts when the pipe below is sound. It is a good fix for a broken ear on an otherwise good flange.

It is not a fix for a pipe that has dropped or for a flange that has rusted away. In those cases the plumber cuts out the old flange and installs a new one. A leaking base can also show up as a general toilet repair call.

DIY or call a plumber?

Many handy homeowners can replace a flange on a slab floor if the drain is easy to reach. Problems start when the drain is cracked, the subfloor is rotten or the flange does not fit the pipe. A mistake can leak sewer gas or water into the floor.

A plumber is the safer choice when the floor is soft, the drain is cast iron or the toilet sits on a second floor. If you are also planning new fixtures, the visit can be combined with plumbing installation work.

Tools and parts for a flange repair

For a basic swap you need a new wax ring or foam seal, new closet bolts, a putty knife, a screwdriver or drill and a level. A flange repair ring and stainless screws help when the old flange is cracked but still firmly attached to the pipe.

Have towels and a bucket ready, and a plastic bag for the old wax. Wear gloves, since the old seal and the drain can be messy. Line the new seal up with the flange so the toilet sits level, then tighten the bolts a little at a time to avoid cracking the porcelain.

Common mistakes that cause repeat leaks

Overtightening the closet bolts is the most common mistake. Porcelain cracks under uneven pressure, and the leak returns. Another is reusing an old wax ring, which has already taken the shape of the old setup and will not seal a new position.

A third mistake is ignoring the floor. If the flange sits on rotten wood, screws will not hold and the toilet will rock again. A plumber checks the wood before reinstalling and repairs the floor where needed.
