Can You Use a Tankless Water Heater in a Corcoran, CA Apartment?

Tankless units save space, which is attractive in an apartment. The catch is power, venting and permission.

Written by the Corcoran Express Plumbing team · Published 2026

Apartments and small rentals often have a small water heater closet, and a tankless unit looks like a way to gain space. It can work, but an apartment adds three questions: who owns the heater, what power and venting the unit needs and whether the building allows the change.

This guide explains how to think about a tankless unit in an apartment, what to check first and how to approach a landlord. It is written for Corcoran, CA renters, owners and property managers.

Who decides: owner, landlord or association

In most rentals the water heater belongs to the landlord, and changes need written approval. In a condo, the association rules and the unit's shared plumbing may limit changes. In a house you own, the decision is yours.

If you are a renter, ask the landlord to approve the work and pay for it. A tankless unit adds value to the property, but it is not a tenant's repair. Get approval in writing before you ask a plumber to start.

Space and venting

A tankless unit mounts on a wall and frees floor space. A gas unit needs a vent that meets code, and the vent path may be hard in an apartment where the heater sits in a closet or interior room. A direct vent unit uses a sealed pipe to the outside.

Electric tankless units need no venting, which makes them attractive for apartments. They do need a heavy electrical load, often several large circuits, and many older apartment panels cannot supply it.

Check these before you plan

  • The heater location and the distance to an exterior wall.
  • Gas or electric supply and its capacity.
  • Panel size and available breakers.
  • Hot-water demand: showers and appliances at once.

Power, gas and the real limit

A whole-apartment electric tankless heater can draw more power than the apartment's panel allows. A point-of-use unit under one sink uses less but serves only that fixture. A gas unit may need a larger gas line than the existing one.

A plumber and, where needed, an electrician can measure what the building can supply. The honest answer is sometimes that a small tank is the better fit. See tankless water heater service for what the plumber checks.

Flow rate and household size

Tankless units are rated by flow in gallons per minute. A one-bath apartment with one or two occupants needs less than a house. If two showers run at once, a small unit may not keep up.

Cooler groundwater in winter makes the unit work harder to reach the set temperature. Ask the plumber to size the unit for winter conditions, not the warmest day.

Maintenance and the landlord conversation

Tankless units need periodic flushing, and the manual says how often. Make sure the lease or management agreement says who handles it. A unit that is never flushed can lose efficiency and fail early.

When you ask the landlord, bring a plumber's written plan, the cost, the expected savings and the maintenance steps. A clear plan makes approval easier. For standard replacement, see water heater installation.

A simple decision guide for renters and owners

If you rent, your first step is a conversation with the landlord. If you own a condo, check the association rules and the building's shared plumbing before you plan. If you own a house, the questions are power, venting and flow.

In every case, a plumber visit can answer the basic questions in an hour. Bring a photo of the heater closet, the breaker panel and the gas meter if you have one.

Costs beyond the unit

The unit is only part of the cost. Venting, a larger gas line, new electrical circuits, a condensate drain and permits all add to the total. In an apartment, these extras can exceed the cost of a simple tank swap.

Ask for a written breakdown that separates the unit from the extras. That lets you compare a tankless plan with a standard replacement fairly.

Small units for a single fixture

If your problem is a kitchen sink far from the heater, a small point-of-use heater under the sink can help. It is not a whole-home heater, but it gives hot water in seconds and is easy to fit in a small space. It needs a nearby outlet and a small circuit.

Point-of-use heaters can be a practical answer when the landlord will not approve a larger change.

Questions for the plumber on the first visit

Ask how many gallons per minute the unit delivers at your winter water temperature, what venting is required and whether your panel and gas line can support it. Ask for a written plan with the total cost.

If the answers show a tankless unit will not fit, ask what small tank or point-of-use options exist so the visit still leads somewhere useful.

Corcoran, CA: renters, soft water and flushing

The City of Corcoran's 2021 water quality report lists average hardness of 52 ppm, which is soft. A tankless unit in this water scales slowly, so the flush interval can be longer than in harder-water cities, though you should follow the manufacturer's manual and the unit's alerts.

If you rent, ask the landlord to approve the work in writing. If you hire a plumber for any install, you can verify the plumber's license on the state board's website before work begins. If you own property in Hanford or another nearby town, see the Hanford plumbing page and the full service area.
